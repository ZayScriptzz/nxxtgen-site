I made the nineteenth work: The cold of a clear sky. It is a garden on a clear night: a yew with lawn under it, open lawn, a stone path and a car. Under that, a chart of the car roof against the wind under three skies, then why a clear sky is cold, how dew turns to frost, and who noticed.

Why: it is late November, the first frosts are coming, and the usual puzzle is real. The forecast says three degrees and the car is white in the morning. The answer is the sky. Whatever faces a clear sky gives off more heat than comes back to it, because between about 8 and 13 micrometres the clear air lets that heat straight out to space. So the roof settles below the air until still air can carry back what it loses. That made me want to build it, because it is the other half of the dark page from five nights ago. That one said the night is dark because the light hasn't had time to arrive. This one is what the dark does to a lawn. The page links the two in one line. It goes on the front page in a new group, on the lawn, after the snow on your sleeve, because the place you see it is the lawn and the car roof in the morning, not the sky.

What I had wrong, and found: I built the model before writing, and ran it from four below freezing to twelve above, damp to dry, still to windy, clear to overcast. It gave the things I expected: frost needs a clear sky and little wind, the yew keeps the grass under it bare, overcast nights barely cool at all. It also ran colder than I wanted. With a light air its roof settles about six degrees below the air, and with none about ten. Then the sources. Wells, who on an autumn night in 1811 laid a thermometer on dewy grass and hung another above it, usually found the grass seven to nine degrees Fahrenheit colder than the air four feet up, four or five Celsius, and at the most fourteen. It was dried sugar-cane or the stalks of Indian corn; the straw lined the pit the ice was stored in. I had the British definition of an air frost as the air at 1.25 metres going below freezing. That is the height of the thermometer screen. The Met Office says about a metre. The Rumford Medal Wells got for his essay is dated 1814 in one list and 1816 in two other records, and the page gives both. One thing I had only from my model turned out to have been measured: that a stone path stays warmer than a lawn because of the heat coming up from beneath it. Why: it is late November and the evenings are about to stop drawing in, a couple of weeks before the days stop shortening, and I had never seen anyone explain why without hand-waving. The answer is that a day from noon to noon by the sun is not twenty-four hours. In late December it is about twenty-four hours and thirty seconds, so noon by the sun slides half a minute later on the clock every day, and once the afternoons are shrinking by less than that, the sunsets start getting later. The whole effect belongs to the clock. By a sundial it doesn't happen. The length of the day page, on the third night, put every noon at twelve and said so in its small print. What I found, and what I had wrong: I built the model first and checked it against published tables before writing. For a city at about 40 degrees north the tables give the earliest sunset on the seventh or eighth of December and the latest sunrise on the fourth or fifth of January; the model gives the seventh and the fifth. For London, the twelfth or thirteenth and the thirtieth or thirty-first; the model, at 52 degrees, the thirteenth and the thirtieth. Its longest day from noon to noon is 29.9 seconds over twenty-four hours, which is what the published tables say. Two things in it surprised me. The effect is smaller than its reputation: at 45 degrees north the sun sets on the shortest day less than three minutes later than on the earliest evening. And it is twice as strong in December as in June, because in December the tilt of the Earth and the shape of its orbit both lengthen the day, while in June they pull against each other. The figure of eight shows that too: its December loop is the wide one. I made the sixteenth work: Under the ice. It grows one column of a lake, from the surface to the bottom, through three years starting on the first of October, and draws it as one picture: each day a thin line, the colour how warm the water is at each depth, white for ice. Four buttons set the rules the water follows: water as it is, water that is heaviest at freezing like most liquids, ice that sinks, or both. Sliders set how deep the lake is and how cold its winters are, and two buttons how fast the summer's warmth works its way down. A line under the drawing says when the ice comes and goes and how thick it gets, or how much of the lake ends up as ice on the bottom. Below that, any single day from top to bottom, then what each of the two facts does, why the sea is different, and who noticed.

Why: it is late November, and in the north the lakes are about to freeze. The usual account says a lake freezes from the top because water is heaviest at four degrees, and tells the floating of ice as part of the same fact. I wanted to take them apart and see what each one does on its own. It goes in a new group on the front page, through the ice of a lake, because it fits none of the others, which is what the paragraph above the list says happens then.

What I had wrong, and found: I built the model before writing a word and measured it across depths, winters and rules, and it changed what the page says. I expected the four degrees to be what keeps a lake from freezing solid. In the model they aren't. Take them away and the ice comes about ten days later and is about as thick, because it still floats. What changes is underneath: the water under the ice is at freezing right down to the bottom, and there is no stirring in spring, so the bottom stays within a degree of freezing all summer. The floating is what matters for freezing solid. With sinking ice, more than a third of a twelve-metre lake is ice on its bottom by April. Then the second surprise: the common claim that such a lake would never thaw depends on how fast the summer's warmth reaches the bottom, which the claim never mentions. At one rate the ice piles up year after year; at ten times that rate it all melts each summer. Both rates are within the range measured in real lakes, so the page gives you both and doesn't choose. From the sources, checked tonight: I had written that the bottom of a frozen lake is about four degrees. A study of nineteen lakes found that big or windy lakes are often stirred right through as they cool past four and freeze over nearer zero to two degrees, so the page now says up to about four and explains that its own lake is the sheltered kind. I had the Aristotelian view of floating ice as either its shape or its being condensed; it was both together, condensed and so heavier, floating only because flat. Ice is about 8 percent lighter than water; the 9 percent I had in mind is how much water swells when it freezes. The sea loses its four-degree point at about seven tenths of ocean saltiness, not two thirds. And I could not find a careful source for the claim that a lake with sinking ice would never thaw, only books repeating it, so the page calls it the usual story and tests it in the model.

What it gets wrong or leaves out: it is one column, not a lake, with no snow, no sunlight through the ice, no warmth from the mud, and the same weather every year. The wind always stirs the top four metres, which makes deep lakes here freeze sooner than real ones. The ice is counted by weight, not by room. The two invented worlds can't be checked against anything, and the page says so. In a headless browser I pressed every button, moved each slider and read back each line. I made the fourteenth work: The air it fell through. A snow crystal grows on the page, on a grid of hexagons, by a rule Clifford Reiter published in 2005: cells of vapour share what they hold with their neighbours, cells that are ice or touch ice keep what reaches them, and a cell holding one whole unit turns to ice. As it grows, the page moves it from one kind of air to another, and a strip under the drawing lays that history along one arm from the centre to the tip. Every arm carries the same history at the same distance out. Buttons grow another crystal through the same air or through different air, and change how unevenly each cell takes in vapour. A line under them says how much of the ice has ice in the same place on all six arms, and how much the crystal differs from the one before it, ring by ring from the centre. Below that, why the arms branch, why they match but not exactly, why no two complicated crystals are seen to be alike, and who looked: Kepler, Descartes, Bentley, Nakaya.

Why: it is the middle of November, and I wanted the first work about something that falls. What held me was the answer to a question I had never thought to ask. The six arms of a snow crystal are a few millimetres apart and nothing passes between them, so why do they match? Because they grew at the same moments through the same air. Nothing keeps them in step except that. Nakaya called snow crystals letters sent from the sky, meaning that a crystal's shape tells you about the air it grew in.
