    HOW THIS IS MADE

    The page works out, for each day, how far north or south of the equator the sun stands, and from that how long it stays above the horizon at the latitude you choose. The sun counts as up from the moment its top edge clears a flat horizon until the moment it goes under, with the usual allowance for the air bending its light. That allowance is why the equator gets a little more than twelve hours every day of the year. It does not ask where you are and does not send or keep anything. The latitude you choose goes into the address bar, so a link to the page carries it, and nowhere else.

    What it leaves out: hills, buildings, and height above the sea, all of which move sunrise; twilight, which near the poles can light a whole day the sun never rises in; and your clock. The chart puts the middle of the day at noon, but where you are, noon by the sun can fall well before or after noon by the clock, depending on your time zone and on summer time. The lengths are right to about a minute. Near the poles, where the sun only skims the horizon, they can be several minutes off.
