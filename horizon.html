How far away the edge of the earth is, from any height.

    FROM THIS HEIGHT

    WHAT IT MEANS

    The edge of a calm sea is closer than it looks. Standing at the water, it is about four and a half kilometres away, an hour's walk if you could walk on it. Climbing does less than you would think. The distance grows with the square root of your height, so to see twice as far you have to go four times as high. A chair adds almost nothing. A lighthouse adds a lot.

    The horizon is also lower than your eyes. From the beach it sits about two and a half minutes of arc below level, less than a twelfth of the width of the moon, which is why it looks flat and level and is neither. You have to be about 260 metres up before it drops a whole moon-width below level. From a plane at cruising height it is more than three degrees down.

    Things past the horizon are not gone. Only their lower parts are hidden, and the page above says how much. That is why a ship going away loses its hull before its mast, and why two high places can see each other across far more than either one's horizon: each has its own, and the line of sight only has to clear the curve in between. The longest view on earth ever photographed was taken in December 2024, from the mountains of northern Turkey to Shkhara, the highest peak in Georgia, 493 kilometres away across the eastern end of the Black Sea.

    HOW THIS IS MADE

    The earth is treated as a smooth ball 6,371 kilometres in radius, and the horizon is the place where a straight line from your eye just touches it. The page gives that distance along the ground, and in a straight line once the two are far enough apart to differ. The second figure allows for the air, which bends light a little way round the curve. Under ordinary conditions this carries the horizon about eight percent further; the page uses the usual allowance and stops giving it above twenty kilometres, where the light that reaches you has passed through air the allowance was not made for. It does not ask where you are and does not send or keep anything. The height you choose goes into the address bar, so a link to the page carries it, and nowhere else.

    What it leaves out: the ground. Every figure here is for a calm sea or a perfectly flat plain at sea level. Hills, waves, haze and your own eyesight all cut the view short, and on land something is usually in the way long before the curve is. The bending of light changes with the weather. Over cold water on a warm day it can lift a coast into view that should be hidden, or squash it flat, and the true horizon can be well nearer or further than the page says. The heights on the buttons are round figures: the space station climbs and sinks by kilometres over a few months, and planes cruise at many heights.
