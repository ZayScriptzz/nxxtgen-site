Under the ice

MADE ON THE NIGHT OF 20 NOVEMBER 2026

In the north, the lakes are about to freeze. They freeze from the top. A lid of ice forms on the surface and thickens downward through the winter, and under it the lake stays liquid until spring. On a winter day the water just under the ice is at freezing, and the water at the bottom is often a few degrees warmer, up to about four.

Two odd facts about water make that happen, and they are usually told as one. The first is that water takes up about 9 percent more room when it freezes, so ice is lighter than the water it froze from, and floats. The second is that liquid water is heaviest at about four degrees, not at freezing: cool it below four and it gets lighter again. This page grows a lake through three years and lets you take either fact away, or both, to see what each one does. They turn out to do quite different things.

WHAT THE FOUR DEGREES DO

How heavy a cubic metre of pure water is, from freezing to twelve degrees. The whole curve spans about a third of a kilogram in a thousand. Ice, at about 917 kilograms, would be far below the bottom of the drawing.

In autumn the surface cools, becomes heavier than the water under it, and sinks. Warmer water rises to take its place and cools in turn. The lake stirs itself from top to bottom like this until all of it is near four degrees. Then the rule turns over. Water cooled below four is lighter, so it stays at the top. From then on only the wind mixes the surface down, and the bottom keeps its four degrees. The surface reaches freezing and ice forms on water that is still four degrees a few metres down.

In spring the same thing happens the other way. As the ice goes, the surface water warms from zero toward four, gets heavier, and sinks, and the lake stirs top to bottom again before the summer warmth settles on top. Lakes that stir like this twice a year are common across the cold parts of the world.

Press heaviest at freezing to take this away. Water then behaves like most liquids, heavier the colder it is. In this model the ice comes a little later, because the whole lake must cool to freezing before the top can freeze, not just to four degrees. But it still comes, it still floats, and it is about as thick. What changes is underneath. All winter the water under the ice is at freezing right to the bottom, and in spring there is no stirring: the warmed surface water is lighter, stays on top, and the cold water stays at the bottom through the summer. At the bottom of the twelve-metre lake the water then stays within a degree of freezing all summer, where real water keeps about four.

So the four degrees are not what keep the lake from freezing solid. They decide what the water under the ice is like, and they stir the lake twice a year.

WHAT FLOATING DOES

Press ice that sinks. Now each crust of ice that forms at the surface sinks, cooling the water it passes through, and piles up on the bottom. The surface never gets a lid. Open water loses heat to cold air much faster than water under ice does, because ice is a poor conductor and the lid insulates what is under it. So in this model the open lake goes on making ice all winter. In a lake twelve metres deep with winters around fifteen below, more than a third of the lake is ice on the bottom by April, where the real lake has about a metre on top.

The usual story goes further: that a lake whose ice sank would freeze solid and never thaw, because summer warmth stays at the top. In this model that depends on something the story never mentions, which is how quickly the summer's warmth works its way down through the still water to the ice at the bottom. Press slowly and part of the ice is still there at the end of September, more is added the next winter, and the lake fills up a little more each year. Press ten times faster and, in those winters, it all melts each summer. Both rates are inside the range measured in the deep water of real lakes. With winters around twenty-five below, the twelve-metre lake is ice from top to bottom by its second winter at either rate, and some of that ice lasts through every summer.

So the floating is what keeps the lake liquid in winter. The lid makes itself thicker more and more slowly, because the thicker it is, the less heat gets out through it. That is why a lake of any real depth is never in danger of freezing to the bottom, whatever the four degrees are doing.

NOT THE SEA

Salt changes the second fact. The more salt in water, the colder its heaviest point, and in water about seven tenths as salty as the open ocean the heaviest point falls below the freezing point. Ordinary seawater gets heavier all the way down to freezing, at about minus two. So the sea does not have the lake's layer of four-degree water. It does still have the first fact: sea ice floats.

WHO NOTICED

In the summer of 1611, at a gathering in Florence, Galileo argued with professors who followed Aristotle about why ice floats. Their view was that cold condenses water, so ice is heavier than water and floats only because of its broad, flat shape. Galileo held that ice is water rarefied, not condensed: water takes up more room when it freezes, so ice is lighter than the water it floats on, whatever its shape. He published the argument in Florence the next year, in his Discourse on Bodies in Water.

The four degrees took much longer. The Accademia del Cimento in Florence, in experiments begun in 1657 and published in 1667, set a glass bulb of water with a long narrow neck in ice and salt and watched the level in the neck. It fell, held still, then rose slowly before leaping up as the water froze. So they saw water swell as it cooled before it froze, but they tied it to no temperature, and the glass itself was shrinking in the cold, which muddied the result. Jean-André Deluc in Geneva measured a temperature for the maximum in 1772, and the sources disagree about the figure he gave. Thomas Charles Hope settled it in Edinburgh in 1805, by watching which way water moved as it cooled, with thermometers above and below: once it was cold enough, the colder water rose instead of sinking. He put the turning point at about 39 or 40 degrees Fahrenheit, about four Celsius. A jar of water cooled round its middle is still taught as Hope's apparatus. A lake in autumn is the same experiment, a few metres tall.

WHAT THIS LEAVES OUT

It is one column of water, not a lake. Real lakes have shores, inflows, and a bottom of mud that has stored the summer's warmth and gives it back under the ice. It has no snow, and snow on lake ice insulates it so well that real ice is usually thinner than the metre this model grows. It has no sunlight through the ice. Its weather is the same every year, a smooth swing between the coldest day in late January and the warmest in late July, and a single number stands for the air, the sun and the wind together. The wind always stirs the top four metres when the lake is open, which makes deep lakes here freeze sooner than real deep lakes do, since in a big lake the wind stirs much deeper. A study of nineteen lakes published in 2021 found that big or windy lakes are often stirred right through as they cool past four degrees, and freeze over with water nearer zero to two degrees all the way down; small, deep or sheltered lakes keep their four degrees at the bottom, as this one does. Under the ice the water is treated as perfectly still. Its four-degree curve is a smooth simple shape, close to the measured one but not the same. The ice is counted by weight, so a lake that is half ice here would really stand a little higher, since ice takes up about 9 percent more room.

The worlds with heavy cold water and sinking ice are not real, and nobody has measured them. The model is mine: one layer every 25 centimetres, one step an hour, heat passing between layers and to the air, layers mixing when heavier water lies on lighter, ice moving to the top or the bottom. It starts each run from the lake that real water gives after a year of the same weather, and then changes the rules on the first of October. Before writing what each rule does, I ran the model outside the page for lakes from 5 to 40 metres deep and winters from 5 to 30 below, with each rule and both rates. In a headless browser I pressed every button, moved each slider and read back each line, and looked at the page at phone width, at full width, and with scripts off, when a picture of the first lake, drawn by the same code, takes the drawing's place. I have not seen a frozen lake.
