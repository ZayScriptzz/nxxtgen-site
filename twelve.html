Why twelve notes

MADE ON THE NIGHT OF 26 NOVEMBER 2026

Pluck a string, then stop it at its middle and pluck the half. The second note is an octave above the first, at twice the rate of vibration, and to most listeners it sounds like the same note again, only higher. That is why the same seven letters come round again and again up a keyboard. Pitch goes round a circle, and one trip round is an octave.

Stop the string two thirds of the way along instead and you get the next simplest sound, a fifth: three vibrations for every two of the first note. Many of the world's scales have a fifth in them, or something close to one. So here is a way to find notes that need nothing but that one sound. Start on C. Go up a fifth, to G. Go up another, to D, and if that has climbed past the octave, bring it down one, which doesn't change what note it is. Keep going. Each fifth carries you 0.585 of the way round the circle.

The question is whether you ever come back to C. You never do, exactly. Every fifth multiplies by three halves and every octave by two, and no number of threes multiplied together ever equals a number of twos: one is always odd and the other even. But some counts come close. The drawing shows the notes you have after any number of fifths, brought into one octave, with C at the top.

The newest note, B♯, lands 23.46 cents past C, nearer than any note before it. The 13 notes cut the octave into steps of three sizes: 4 of 113.69 cents, 8 of 90.22 cents and 1 of 23.46 cents.

TWO SIZES OF STEP

Look at the ring between the notes rather than at the notes. After four fifths there are five notes, C, G, D, A and E, and they cut the octave into steps of only two sizes. Those five are a pentatonic scale, the one you get from the black keys of a piano. After six fifths there are seven notes, again in steps of only two sizes, five large and two small, and that is the pattern of the white keys: whole tones and half tones. After eleven fifths there are twelve notes, still in two sizes, now both about half a tone. The twelfth fifth lands just past C, at the note called B sharp, and splits one of the larger half tones into a smaller one and a sliver.

The sliver is 23.46 cents, where a cent is a hundredth of a half tone on a piano, so it is a little under a quarter of a half tone. It has a name, the Pythagorean comma. Twelve fifths overshoot seven octaves by the ratio 531,441 to 524,288, which is twelve threes multiplied together over nineteen twos multiplied together.

Stepping round a circle by the same amount every time always cuts it into gaps of at most three sizes, and when there are three, the largest is the other two put together. That was proved in the late 1950s. For the fifth, the counts that leave exactly two are 2, 3, 5, 7, 12, 17, 29, 41 and 53. Five, seven and twelve notes are the scales most music built on fifths is made of. The next good return to C after twelve takes forty-one fifths, which miss by 19.84 cents, and then fifty-three, which miss by only 3.62.

SHARING OUT THE SLIVER

Twelve fifths almost close the circle. To make them close exactly, take a twelfth of the comma off every fifth, about 1.96 cents each, and the twelve notes come out evenly spaced, 100 cents apart. That is equal temperament, and it is what most pianos, guitars and electronic instruments are tuned to now. Every key is then like every other, and a piece can start on any note. Its fifths are so close to pure that few people can hear the difference.

The cost falls on the other sounds. After the octave and the fifth, the next simplest is the major third, five vibrations to four, the sound at the bottom of a major chord. Four of the equal steps make 400 cents, and a pure major third is 386.31, so the piano's thirds are 13.69 cents wide. That can be heard. Nothing forces twelve, either. Divide the octave into any number of equal steps and some step will be the nearest to a fifth and some the nearest to a third. HOW YOU HEAR IT

Two notes a little out of tune with each other beat: the sound swells and fades, slowly if they are close, faster as they draw apart. A plucked or bowed note is not one rate of vibration but a stack of them, the note itself and fainter ones at two, three, four and five times its rate. The third in C's stack and the second in G's are the same pitch when the fifth is pure, so they ring together. When the fifth is the piano's, they miss by a little under one vibration a second, at the C in the middle of the keyboard, and the fifth beats slowly, once every second or so. For a major third the notes that meet are the fifth in the lower stack and the fourth in the upper. On a piano those miss by about ten a second, a fast flutter that is part of what a piano's major chord sounds like. Hermann von Helmholtz, in 1863, made this the centre of his account of why some pairs of notes sound smooth and others rough.

The sounds are made in your browser, only when you press a button, and stop when you leave the page. Each note is built from its first eight partials, the stack described above, so that the beats have something to happen between. They start on the C in the middle of the keyboard, 261.63 vibrations a second.

WHO NOTICED

The oldest surviving telling is the story of Pythagoras passing a smithy and hearing hammers that rang in octaves, fifths and fourths, then finding their weights in the proportions 12, 9, 8 and 6. It is in the Manual of Harmonics of Nicomachus, written around AD 100, and it is wrong as physics: a hammer's weight doesn't set its note that way. The same story says the weights also work hung from strings. In 1589 Vincenzo Galilei, the father of Galileo, showed that they don't: to sound a fifth, the weights on two equal strings must be in the proportion 9 to 4, the square of 3 to 2. Historians still argue over how much of that he tried by hand.

In China, Jing Fang, who lived from 78 to 37 BC, carried the chain of fifths on to sixty notes, and he is credited with noticing that fifty-three of them come back very near the start, the 3.62-cent miss in the drawing. Its usual modern name is Mercator's comma, after Nicholas Mercator in seventeenth-century England, who divided the octave into fifty-three. In 1872 and 1873 a harmonium with fifty-three notes to the octave was built for R. H. M. Bosanquet.

Sharing out the comma evenly was worked out exactly twice within about twenty years, at opposite ends of the world. Zhu Zaiyu, a prince of the Ming dynasty, gave the twelve equal steps as numbers in a book whose preface is dated 1584, with the twelfth root of two worked out to more than twenty digits. Simon Stevin in the Netherlands wrote out the same scheme in a manuscript of around 1600 to 1605 that was not printed until 1884. Marin Mersenne printed string lengths for it in Paris in 1636. Scholars argue over how much credit each should get. Christiaan Huygens in 1691 proposed thirty-one equal steps instead, for thirds that are nearly pure, and a thirty-one-note organ built for Adriaan Fokker was opened in Haarlem in 1950; it is in Amsterdam now. Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier, dated 1722, says in its title that the keyboard is well tempered. What tuning he meant by that is still argued over. Pianos came late to equal temperament: in England the maker Broadwood took it up around 1846.

The cent is Alexander Ellis's, brought in during the 1880s and used in his paper of 1885 on the scales of many nations, which measured instruments from many countries and concluded that there is no one musical scale, and nothing natural about any of them, only great variety.

HOW THIS IS MADE

Everything in the drawings and the lines under them is worked out on the page from two numbers, the fifth as three to two and the major third as five to four, and the same code wrote the versions you see with scripts off. A real instrument is not so tidy. A piano's strings are stiff, so the partials above each note run a little sharp, and tuners make the octaves slightly wider than two to one to match; nothing on the page does that. The sounds are made from eight partials of fixed strength, which is not any instrument. The page treats the fifth and the major third as the sounds that matter, which is true of the music the twelve notes were made for and not of all music. A Javanese gamelan, for one, is tuned set by set to its own scales, with no fixed standard from one gamelan to the next. The three sizes of gap come from a theorem conjectured by Hugo Steinhaus and first proved by Vera Sós, and others, around 1957 and 1958. The history above is from standard references and the studies they cite. I have not read Nicomachus, Zhu Zaiyu or Ellis myself. The page keeps nothing and sends nothing.
