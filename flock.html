Seven neighbours

MADE ON THE NIGHT OF 14 NOVEMBER 2026

On winter evenings, before they settle for the night, starlings gather over their roost and fly together as one shape, thickening, thinning, folding over itself, for twenty minutes or half an hour. There may be a few thousand of them or several hundred thousand. Nobody leads. Each bird steers by a few of the birds around it, and the shape is what that adds up to.

The flock below is a model of that, flat where a real one is deep. Each bird does three things, and only with the birds it is watching: it keeps from hitting them, it turns to fly the way they are flying, and it drifts toward where they are. A faint pull toward the middle stands in for the roost. One bird is drawn in blue, with a line to each bird it is watching.

A still from the model: six hundred birds, each watching its seven nearest.

A NUMBER, NOT A DISTANCE

The three rules are the ones Craig Reynolds gave his simulated birds in 1987, which he called boids: avoid collisions, match velocity, stay close to the flock. His birds paid attention to every other bird within a certain distance, and for twenty years most models of flocks did the same. It is the obvious guess. It was hard to check against real birds, because almost nobody had measured where each bird in a large flock was.

In the winter of 2005 to 2006 a team in Rome found out. They set up cameras near the starling roost at Termini railway station and photographed the flocks from more than one angle at the same instant, which let them work out the position of every bird in three dimensions. They reconstructed ten flocks this way, the largest about 2,600 birds, and looked at how each bird placed itself among the birds around it, and how far that reached. The answer was not a distance. Each bird was steering by its six or seven nearest neighbours, however near or far those happened to be. In a loose part of the flock the seventh might be five metres away; in a dense part, one. What mattered was how many birds were between, not how many metres.

The authors argued that this is what holds a flock together when a falcon tears into it and the birds suddenly spread out. A bird that keeps to a distance loses its neighbours exactly when it needs them most. A bird that keeps to a number never does. You can try it above. Choose every bird within a distance. At the nine pixels it starts with, the average bird is watching somewhere near as many others as before, but the average hides the edges: there are always a few birds with nobody in range, wandering on their own. Now scatter them. Where the flock thins, the count of birds watching nobody climbs into the dozens, and some of them take a long while to find their way back. Go back to a number of birds and scatter them again. However thin the flock gets, every bird still has its seven to steer by.

Seven is not a law. It is what that study measured, as an average, in one kind of bird. In this model, watching even one neighbour makes a loose flock, with a ragged fringe of ten or so birds outside it at any moment. Three makes it tidier. By about seven, adding more makes little difference that I could measure. Watching none, the birds are a cloud of separate birds, held near each other only by the pull of the roost.

HOW FAST A TURN TRAVELS

When one edge of a flock turns, the turn passes through it like a ripple. The same group later filmed flocks turning and timed it. The turn crossed the flock at twenty to forty metres a second, while the birds themselves were flying at seven to twelve. Across a flock of four hundred it took a little over half a second. Another of their studies found that when one bird strays from the flock's average heading, birds right across the flock tend to stray the same way, not just its neighbours, and that the reach of this grows with the size of the flock: as far as they could measure, there is no distance beyond which a bird stops mattering. Each starling watches seven, and every starling is affected by all the others.

WHY THEY DO IT

Nobody is certain, but the best evidence points at falcons. In 2017 a group at a university in England gathered reports of more than three thousand murmurations sent in by the public. The flocks averaged about 30,000 birds, the largest reported was 750,000, and a display lasted about 26 minutes on average. They grew from October to early February and then shrank until March. Where a bird of prey was about, the flocks were larger and flew for longer, and the display was more likely to end with the whole flock dropping into the roost together. The authors read that as safety in numbers, rather than the birds gathering to keep warm.

When a peregrine attacks, dark bands often run through the flock, always away from the falcon and often faster than the flock is flying. A study of filmed attacks, published in 2011, found that the falcons caught birds less often when the flock made these waves. They seem to start with a few birds swerving away, and spread as each bird's neighbours swerve after it. Draw the pointer slowly through the model flock and you can sometimes see a faint version of it.

WHAT THE MODEL GETS WRONG

It is flat. A real flock is three-dimensional, and usually thin and wide, so it darkens when you see it edge on and pales when it turns to face you; much of what makes a murmuration beautiful is that, and none of it is here. It has a few hundred birds, not tens of thousands. How hard each bird steers by each rule is my choice, set by eye until it looked like flying: the research says which birds a starling watches, not how strongly it responds to them. A real bird also cannot see through a wall of other birds, and some later models pick neighbours by what a bird can see rather than by count. The falcon is only a point the birds run from. A real one picks out a single bird. Distances in the model are in pixels, and the pixels are not metres.

The figures are from Ballerini and others, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2008 (the six or seven neighbours); Attanasi and others, Nature Physics, 2014 (the turns); Cavagna and others, the same Proceedings, 2010 (the reach); Goodenough and others, PLoS ONE, 2017 (the murmurations reported by the public); and Procaccini and others, Animal Behaviour, 2011 (the waves). In Britain, where most of those reports came from, starlings are on the red list of birds of conservation concern: the birds that breed there have fallen by more than three quarters since the 1970s, and the winter flocks are swollen by birds from northern Europe. The murmurations are still there. Fewer birds are making them.
