How long is the coast

MADE ON THE NIGHT OF 13 NOVEMBER 2026

Take a pair of dividers, the kind with two points, and open them to a hundred kilometres on the scale of a map. Put one point on the coast of Great Britain and walk them round the island, step after step, each time swinging the free point forward until it touches the coast again, and count the steps. Thirty steps and a little over: about three thousand kilometres.

Now close the dividers to ten kilometres and walk round again. This time the steps go into bays and estuaries that the long ruler stepped straight across, and the island comes out at more than five thousand. Close them further and it grows again. A smooth curve doesn't do this. Measure a circle with a shorter and shorter ruler and its length creeps up to a limit and stops there. The coast doesn't stop. Every bay has smaller bays in it, all the way down to rocks and pebbles, so every shorter ruler finds more coast to go round.

LONGER AND LONGER

Do it for every ruler from four hundred kilometres down to one, and set the lengths against the rulers on scales where each step is ten times the last. The points come out close to a straight line, falling to the right: each time the ruler gets ten times shorter, the coast gets about 1.8 times longer, and it doesn't level off until the map itself runs out of detail. For a smooth curve the line would lie flat.

The dashed line falls at the slope Lewis Fry Richardson found for the west coast of Britain. In the shaded strip on the left, the map runs out.

Between rulers of ten and two hundred kilometres the points on this outline fall at very nearly the slope that the dashed line shows, a slope found more than sixty years ago for a different map of part of the same coast. It is the number this page is really about. Not the length, which depends on the ruler, but how fast the length grows as the ruler shrinks. That is a property of the coast itself.

WHO NOTICED

The mathematician Hugo Steinhaus wrote in 1954 that the left bank of the Vistula, measured with more and more care, would give lengths ten, a hundred, even a thousand times as great as the map's. In the same years the English physicist Lewis Fry Richardson was working on a stranger question. He had spent years counting wars, and he wanted to know whether two countries that share a longer border are more likely to fight. To find out he needed the lengths of borders, and he found that the published ones disagreed. As the story is usually told, Portugal gave its border with Spain as 987 kilometres, and Spain gave the same border as 1,214.

Richardson measured coasts and borders on maps himself, with the ruler set to several lengths, and plotted the results the way the chart above does. Each came out close to a straight line, and the lines fell at different slopes. The coast of South Africa, one of the smoothest in the atlas, barely fell at all. The west coast of Britain, one of the most broken, fell the most steeply of all he measured. The work was published in 1961, eight years after he died, as a paper subtitled an appendix to his book about wars.

In 1967 Benoit Mandelbrot took Richardson's slopes and gave them a meaning in a short paper titled How long is the coast of Britain? A smooth line has one dimension, and a filled-in shape has two. A coast, he argued, sits in between: so crinkled at every scale that it partly fills the plane. How steeply its line falls, plus one, measures how much. South Africa's coast came out at about 1.02, hardly more than a line. The west coast of Britain came out at 1.25. Eight years later he coined a word for shapes like that: fractal.

The outline on this page gives about 1.26, for the whole island, between rulers of ten and two hundred kilometres. I didn't expect it to land so close to his figure for the west coast. It is a different map, measured a different way, and the east coast is smoother than the west, so I take the closeness as partly luck.

THE FIGURES PEOPLE GIVE

So every published length of a coast is the length for some ruler, and the ruler is almost never stated. The Ordnance Survey, which maps Great Britain at great detail, gives the coast of the main island as about 17,820 kilometres, and about 31,370 with its islands. The CIA's World Factbook gives the whole United Kingdom, with Northern Ireland and the islands, as 12,429. The World Resources Institute gives the United Kingdom as 19,717. None of these is a mistake. They are answers to different questions, asked with different rulers.

WHAT THIS MAP CAN'T SHOW

The outline here is itself a measurement with a ruler. It is a world map drawn for printing at about one to ten million, and its points are mostly a kilometre or two apart. Between its points it runs straight. So below a ruler of about three kilometres the walk stops finding new coast, and on the chart the points level off. That is this map running out, not the coast. At a ruler of one kilometre the page counts about 7,080 kilometres, less than half the Ordnance Survey's figure, which comes from mapping far finer than this.

Where you start matters too, more than I expected. With a long ruler the steps fall differently from different starting points, and the line under the slider gives the spread from forty starting points round the island. With a hundred-kilometre ruler it is about 2,730 to 3,090 kilometres. With short rulers the starting point hardly matters.

And there is the question of where the coast is at all. The sea comes in and goes out twice a day, so a coastline is drawn at some agreed level of the tide, and not every map agrees on which. Sand moves, cliffs fall, and marshes are neither land nor sea. The finer the ruler, the more these choices decide the answer, until somewhere below the size of a pebble the question stops meaning anything.

The outline is the main island of Great Britain from Natural Earth's land data at 1:10 million, which is in the public domain; smaller islands are left out. It is drawn on a transverse Mercator projection centred on two degrees west, which with the Earth taken as a sphere keeps distances on the island true to within a few tenths of a percent. The dividers step along the outline as a chain of straight lines; each step ends where the coast next crosses a circle of the ruler's radius, and what is left at the end, less than one step, is measured straight and added.
