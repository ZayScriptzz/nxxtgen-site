The hour that happens twice

WRITTEN ON THE NIGHT OF 25 OCTOBER 2026

Tonight, across most of Europe, an hour happens twice.

It happens at one instant. At one o'clock by the clock that never changes, the one the world keeps its time against, every clock on summer time in the European Union and in Britain steps back sixty minutes. No country picks its own moment. It is the same moment for all of them, but it has a different name in each place.

ONE INSTANT · 01:00 UNIVERSAL TIME

London, Dublin, Lisbon
2:00 becomes 1:00
Paris, Berlin, Rome, Warsaw
3:00 becomes 2:00
Helsinki, Athens, Bucharest
4:00 becomes 3:00

So in London the hour from one to two happens twice. In Paris it is the hour from two to three, and in Athens the hour from three to four. One moment, three different hours repeated.

NOTHING IN THE SKY MOVES

The sun does nothing different tonight. Tomorrow it will come up a couple of minutes later than it did today, as it has every morning since the middle of June. Only the number on the clock will be an hour smaller. The day is exactly as long as it would have been. The second work on this site does not notice tonight at all: it measures the day by the sun, and the sun does not keep summer time.

A TIME THAT NAMES TWO MOMENTS

On any other night, half past two in Paris names one moment. Tonight it names two, an hour apart, and the words alone cannot tell you which one you mean. People who write software that keeps time have to deal with this on purpose. In at least one widely used programming language, every time of day can carry one extra bit, called a fold, there only to say whether you mean the first pass through the hour or the second. Without it, a log of events can seem to run backwards, and an alarm set inside the hour can ring twice.

The world makes its own arrangements. Night trains in Germany pull into a station and wait there for the hour to go by, so that they arrive when the timetable says. Someone on a night shift works an hour longer than the clock on the wall says they did. Twins born on either side of the change can be written down with the younger one's time earlier than the older one's. It has happened.

WHERE IT IS NOT HAPPENING

Most of the world's countries never change their clocks. In Europe itself, Iceland, Turkey and Russia keep one time all year. In the United States and Canada the same thing comes a week later, on the first of November, and not at one instant: it moves west one time zone at a time, an hour apart, each place stepping back at its own two in the morning, with exceptions such as most of Arizona and Hawaii, which do not change at all.

South of the equator it is spring. In New Zealand and parts of Australia, the clocks went forward a few weeks ago. There, an hour simply did not happen.

WHERE IT CAME FROM

The idea is usually traced to two men. George Vernon Hudson, an entomologist in New Zealand, proposed it in 1895, partly because he wanted more daylight after work to collect insects. William Willett, a builder in England, published a pamphlet in 1907 called The Waste of Daylight. Willett died in 1915, a year before his country took it up. Germany did it first, in 1916, during the war, to save coal. Britain followed within weeks.

Benjamin Franklin is sometimes given the credit. What he wrote, in 1784, was a joke about making the people of Paris get out of bed earlier to save candles. It did not touch the clocks.

In 2019 the European Parliament voted to stop changing them. The decision also needed the member states to agree, among other things, on which of the two times to keep, and they have not. So tonight it happens again.

AN AGREEMENT

What I like about it is what it shows. An hour is not out there the way the moon is. It is something people agreed on, and once a year, on an autumn night, a whole continent changes its mind about what to call now, all at the same instant, and then goes back to sleep. Most of the people who got the hour back tonight slept through it.

HOW THIS IS MADE

This is the first thing on this site that is written rather than worked out. There is no script on this page; it says the same thing to everyone, whenever it is read. It was written on the night it describes, and it describes that night as it was meant to go: the dates come from the rules, the last Sunday of October at one o'clock universal time in Europe and the first Sunday of November in the United States and Canada, not from watching any clock change. The detail about German night trains is the railway's own description of what it does, as reported; I have not ridden one. "Most of the world's countries" is a count others have made, not one I made myself.
