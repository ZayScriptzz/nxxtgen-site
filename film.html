Black before it breaks

MADE ON THE NIGHT OF 28 NOVEMBER 2026

Dip a wire frame in washing-up water, lift it out, and hold it upright in front of something dark with a lamp behind you. For a few seconds the film in the frame is clear and pale. Then colours start at the top, in level bands that spread down the frame: yellow, red, blue, green, pink, and again. The bands slide slowly downward. A silvery white band forms under the top wire, and above it, last of all, comes a patch that looks black, as if the film had a hole in it. It has no hole. That part is the thinnest the film ever gets, and some time after it appears, the film breaks.

None of the colour is in the soap. The film is clear. Every colour in it comes from how thick the film is at that height, and the drawing below works out each one from that alone.

At the marked height it is 570 nanometres thick. Of the most it could reflect of each, it is reflecting 77 percent of red light, 47 percent of green and 90 percent of blue.

TWO REFLECTIONS

A soap film is a thin layer of water held between two layers of soap. Light falling on it is reflected twice: about two percent at the front face and about two percent at the back. The light from the back face has crossed the film and come back, so it comes out a little behind the light from the front, by twice the thickness of the film, and inside water light goes about a quarter slower than in air, which stretches the delay further. Where the two reflections come out in step they add, and the film reflects that colour brightly. Where they come out half a wave apart they cancel, and that colour goes on through the film instead.

Each colour of light has its own length of wave, from about 400 millionths of a millimetre for violet to about 700 for red, so a film of one thickness puts some colours in step and others out. Daylight and lamplight have all of them. What the film reflects is what is left once the ones it cancels are taken out, and that is the colour you see. A few tens of nanometres thicker or thinner, and a different set cancels. So the bands are lines of equal thickness, like the contours on a map, and the colour of each tells you how thick the film is there.

WHY THE THINNEST IS BLACK

This is the part that seems backwards. As the film thins toward nothing, the delay between the two reflections shrinks toward nothing too, so you might expect them to come out in step for every colour at once, and the thinnest film to be the brightest white of all. It is the darkest. The two reflections are not alike. At the front face the light is going from air into water and comes back turned over, as a wave does when it meets something denser. At the back face it is going from water into air and comes back the right way up. So with no delay at all they are exactly half a wave apart, and every colour cancels. A film much thinner than a wave of light reflects almost nothing, and what you see through it is the dark behind.

How dark depends on how thin. In the drawing the black film is 12 nanometres thick, close to the average Reinold and Rücker measured in 1883, and it reflects about three and a half percent as much green light as the brightest band does, so the screen shows it as a very dark grey. Black films come in two kinds. The thicker, called common black film, has been measured at anywhere from about 15 to 70 nanometres, depending on the soap and on any salt in the water, and at the thick end of that it would look grey rather than black. The thinner, called Newton black film, is about 4 nanometres, little more than the two layers of soap with almost no water between them, and reflects less than half a percent. Newton saw something like the pair: inside his black spots, he wrote, were smaller round spots much blacker still. The edge between the black and the colours is sharp. The film does not fade into black; it steps down into it, and Reinold and Rücker found the black part even in thickness right across. In the drawing the step is from 50 nanometres straight down to 12. The 50 is my setting.

WHY THE COLOURS RUN OUT

At the bottom of a fresh film, and anywhere it is more than a thousandth of a millimetre or so thick, the colours fade toward a pale grey. A thick film still puts some colours in step and others out, but now the ones it keeps and the ones it cancels alternate many times across the spectrum: some red, no orange, some yellow, no green, and so on. The eye adds that up as a mixture with a little of everything in it, and sees white. On the chart above the three lines drift in and out of step with each other as the film thickens, and the colour under them pales. That is also why the colours of a film come in a fixed order, the same on every bubble, which Newton wrote down as a table more than three hundred years ago. His last, seventh order is a reddish white, at about a thousandth and a half of a millimetre of water.

LOOKING FROM THE SIDE

Move your head and the bands move. Light that crosses the film at a slant has further to go between the faces, but the light from the front face has also gone further by the time the two meet, and the difference between them comes out shorter, as if the film were thinner. So seen from the side, every colour moves to where the film is thicker, which on an upright film is downward. The second slider under the drawing does that. At 60 degrees from straight on, each colour sits where the film is about a third thicker than where you saw it face on. A film also reflects more light at a slant, which the drawing hides by turning its brightness down as your eye would.

WHY IT THINS, AND BREAKS

Water runs downhill, including the water between the two layers of soap, so a film held upright drains, thinnest at the top and thicker toward the bottom wire. Gravity is only part of it. Where the film meets the wire there is a thicker, curved border of liquid, and its curve draws water out of the film. In a film of ordinary washing-up liquid much of the thinning happens there, at the edges: patches of thinner film are pulled out of the border and drift up, and thicker film is drawn in. It is called marginal regeneration, and the standard account of it is a book on the thinning of soap films by Mysels, Shinoda and Frankel from 1959, which I know only through later papers. Water also evaporates from both faces. Once the top is thin enough, black film appears there and spreads, and in the end the film breaks, at a moment I found no way to predict. The drawing does none of this. Each height in it thins at a steady rate of its own, faster toward the top, a rule I chose because it makes bands that start at the top and slide down, as real ones do. The film is three thousandths of a millimetre thick when it is lifted out, also my choice, and it breaks at four minutes because I set it to.

WHO NOTICED

Robert Hooke described the colours of thin flakes of mica in Micrographia in 1665, and mentioned in passing the bubbles children blow with soapy water. In March 1672 he showed the Royal Society a bubble that, after its colours, turned white, and in that white film, he wrote, there appeared several holes. They were the black film, and he took them for holes, as anyone would. Newton, in his Opticks of 1704, blew bubbles and covered each with a clear glass to keep the air from stirring it. He watched the colours form rings round the top and descend in order to the bottom, and saw a small round black spot appear at the top, sometimes growing to half or three quarters of an inch across. He set the colours out as a table of thicknesses, seven orders of them, ending in white, and gave the thickness where a film looks very black as three eighths of a millionth of an inch, about 9.5 nanometres. He explained the orders by what he called fits of easy reflection and easy transmission, a rhythm he gave to the light itself. Thomas Young explained them as waves. In a paper read to the Royal Society in 1802 he wrote that light reflected from a thinner medium must be supposed retarded by half the interval, “for instance, in the central black spot of a soap-bubble”. To test it he put oil of sassafras between a flint glass and a crown glass, so that both reflections went into something denser and were alike, and predicted that the centre would then be white instead of black. It was. Joseph Plateau, in his book on liquids of 1873, gave the mixture for a film that lasts: three parts of soap solution to two of glycerine. Reinold and Rücker revised Newton's table in 1881 and measured the black film in 1883. And over the New Year of 1889 to 1890, C. V. Boys gave three lectures on soap bubbles to an audience of children at the London Institution, printed as Soap-Bubbles and the Forces Which Mould Them. A chapter on their colours was added only in 1911.

HOW THIS IS MADE

The colours are worked out on the page from the thickness and nothing else. The film is taken as water, bending light as Daimon and Masumura measured it at room temperature in 2007, with air on both sides. The soap layers are left out, and so is glycerine, which bends light more and would move every colour to a slightly thinner film. Both reflections and every further bounce between the faces are counted, for light of 61 wavelengths from 400 to 700 nanometres in equal amounts, averaged over the two polarisations, and added up as the eye would, using a fit made by Wyman, Sloan and Shirley in 2013 to the standard observer of 1931. A film too thick for colours is shown at the same pale grey at every angle. A screen can't show the brightest of the colours, and they are clipped. As a check: the first white here comes at about 100 nanometres, and Newton's table gives 98 for water. The first red comes at about 180, where Newton gives 171 and Reinold and Rücker about 213, so the two old tables disagree by more than mine disagrees with either. Their second-order colours sit 10 to 25 nanometres thicker than mine. The thinning, the starting thickness, the step down to black and the moment it breaks are made up, and the page says so where they appear. The history was checked against the old texts themselves where they could be found: Newton's Opticks, Young's two papers of 1802, Hooke's account as printed in Birch's history of the Royal Society, Reinold and Rücker's papers, Plateau and Boys. The same code wrote the drawings you see with scripts off, at ninety seconds, seen straight on. The page keeps nothing and sends nothing.
