Why thunder rumbles

MADE ON THE NIGHT OF 27 NOVEMBER 2026

A flash of lightning is over in a fraction of a second. Its thunder can go on for twenty. The whole channel, several kilometres of it, is heated at almost the same instant, so every part of it makes its sound at once. But the parts are at different distances from you, and sound is slow, about a kilometre every three seconds. So you hear the nearest part first and the farthest last, and everything in between in order. The rumble is the bolt, laid out by its distance from you.

The drawing is one made-up bolt, seen from the side, 20 kilometres across. It strikes the ground at the small triangle and runs on through the cloud. The faint circles round you are the distances sound covers in each second after the flash. Each part of the channel is coloured by when its sound reaches you, palest first and deepest last, and the dashed line goes to the part you hear first. The circled stretch makes the loudest moment.

The loudest moment comes 16.3 seconds after the flash. Most of it is from a stretch of the channel inside the cloud, 5.4 km from you and 5.2 km up, lying within 11 degrees of square to you, so all of it is nearly the same distance away.

CLAPS

Thunder is not a smooth roar. It has sudden claps in it, and the drawing shows where they come from. A stretch of channel that lies square to you, across your line of sight, is all about the same distance away, so its whole sound arrives in the same instant. A stretch that points toward you spreads its sound out, its near end first and its far end later, and you hear it as part of the roll. The channel is crooked on every scale, so as the circles sweep out across it they cross stretches lying every way, and the claps fall wherever they happen to cross one square on.

I measured that in the model before writing it here. Across 40 bolts and five places to stand, about a fifth of the channel lies within 15 degrees of square to the listener. Of the sound in the sharpest claps, the moments that stand furthest above the rumble around them, more than half comes from stretches that do. That is only from the timing. A real straight stretch also sends most of its sound out sideways, square to itself, which makes the same stretches louder still. The model leaves that out.

COUNTING

The old way to tell how far off a storm is: count the seconds from the flash to the thunder and divide by three for kilometres, or by five for miles. Light takes a few hundred-thousandths of a second to cross the distance, so the flash is, for this purpose, the moment it happened. Sound at 20 degrees goes 343 metres a second, a kilometre in 2.9 seconds, a little slower in colder air.

What the count measures is the distance to the nearest part of the channel, not to where the bolt struck. Those can be kilometres apart. A bolt can run for miles inside the cloud before it comes down, so the first thunder you hear may come from overhead while the strike was well away, or the other way round. Move along the ground in the drawing and watch the dashed line: sometimes it goes to the strike, often to somewhere up in the cloud.

WHY IT STOPS

Thunder is rarely heard more than about twenty-five kilometres away, though the flash can be seen much farther. On a stormy afternoon the air usually gets colder with height, and sound is slower in cold air. So the part of each sound wave that is higher up lags behind the part lower down, and the wave tilts and bends upward, away from the ground. From any height there is one path that only just grazes the ground, and beyond where it touches down, sound from that height passes over your head. Sound from the top of the bolt reaches farthest. Past where its grazing path comes down, no part of the bolt can reach you directly: you are in the sound shadow, and a flash seen with no thunder is what is often called heat lightning.

The drawing shows the grazing paths for each kilometre of height, and the one from the top of the bolt in blue. It is stretched upward one and a half times so the paths can be seen to bend. Change how fast the air cools with height, and the shadow moves. In air that gets warmer with height, which happens on still winter nights and over cold ground, sound bends back down, and there is no shadow at all.

With the air cooling 6.5 degrees for every kilometre up, sound from the top of this bolt, 5.3 km up, can reach the ground directly as far as 30 km away. Where you stand, 6.0 km from it, sound from below 0.2 km passes over your head, and 99 percent of the channel can reach you.

The shadow also changes what you hear from closer in. Move yourself out past fifteen kilometres in the first drawing and the low parts of the channel go grey, because their sound has gone over your head. What is left is the part high in the cloud, farther off, and the thunder gets shorter and quieter as well as later.

WHY FAR THUNDER IS LOW

Close to, thunder is a crack, a sharp tearing sound. Farther away it is a low roll. Air soaks up high pitches far more than low ones, so over a few kilometres the crack is filtered out of the sound and the low part goes on. Most of the sound of thunder is low to start with. Measurements differ from storm to storm, but they put most of it below about 80 vibrations a second, under the lowest notes most bass singers can reach, and some of it is too low to hear at all. Where that lowest part comes from is still argued over.

WHAT MAKES THE SOUND

The flash heats the air of the channel to about 30,000 degrees, five times hotter than the surface of the Sun, in a few millionths of a second, too fast for the air to move out of the way first. For a moment the pressure in the channel is ten times the air's around it or more, and the channel bursts outward faster than sound, as a shock wave. Within a short distance that weakens into ordinary sound, and the sound is the thunder. The air in the channel cools and settles back afterwards, but thunder is not, as was once said, air rushing back into a gap the lightning left.

WHO LISTENED

Aristotle, in his Meteorology in the fourth century BC, had thunder as dry vapour driven out of a cloud and striking the clouds nearby, and lightning as that vapour catching fire. He knew the flash is seen first, and gave the right reason, that sight is quicker than hearing, with the example of a trireme seen from a distance, whose oars are already coming back before the sound of their striking the water arrives. He drew the wrong conclusion from it: that the thunder comes first, and the flash is only seen sooner. Lucretius, in Rome in the first century BC, made the same point with a man felling a tree a long way off, his axe seen to strike before the blow is heard.

Counting thunder is a way of measuring a distance with the speed of sound. Run backwards, with a gun fired at a known distance and the time from flash to bang, it measures the speed of sound, and that is how it was first done, in the 1600s. Marin Mersenne, in a book of 1636, and Pierre Gassendi, or someone measuring for him, both got figures well over 400 metres a second, too fast. Members of the Accademia del Cimento in Florence got about 350. In 1738, with cannon fired between Montmartre and Montlhéry outside Paris, members of the French Academy got 332 metres a second at freezing, within a fraction of a percent of the modern figure. A newspaper column in 1999 credits Gustave-Adolphe Hirn, in 1888, with the explanation of thunder as the channel's air suddenly heated and swelling, and with saying that the start of the thunder gives the distance to the nearest part and its length the length of the channel. I have found that only as the column retells it.

In 1949 Robert Fleagle, working from how temperature and wind change with height, explained why thunder is seldom heard beyond about twenty-five kilometres. His example was air at 30 degrees at the ground, cooling at 9.8 degrees a kilometre, with sound from five kilometres up; the drawing above gives 24 kilometres for the same height on a hot afternoon. In the late 1960s and 1970s Arthur Few, at Rice University in Texas, worked out where the rumble and the claps come from, much as this page does, and added that each straight stretch of channel sends its sound mostly sideways. He recorded thunder on several microphones set out near one another and, from the direction each moment of thunder came from and when it arrived, traced the sound back to draw the channel in three dimensions. Channels drawn that way in later storms, by Few and his colleagues, ran mostly sideways, in layers two or three kilometres thick inside the cloud.

HOW YOU HEAR IT

The sound is made in your browser from noise, only when you press a button, and stops when you leave the page. Its loudness at each moment follows the chart, and the higher pitches are taken out more the farther away that moment's sound has come from. It is not a recording. Small speakers can't play the lowest part of it, which is most of real thunder. Flash, then wait for it lights the bolt and makes you wait the real time before the sound arrives; from far off that is more than a minute.

HOW THIS IS MADE

The bolt is made up. It is a crooked walk in steps of 15 metres, each step turned from the last by a random amount, from the ground up to between 4 and 5.5 kilometres, then 3 to 8 kilometres one way through the cloud and a shorter run the other way, with two to four branches that end in the air. I set how crooked it is by eye. Afterwards I found a measurement of real channels, published in 1968 and known to me only as others cite it, that gives an average turn of about 16 degrees from one short piece to the next; the model's average is 15.7, which is partly luck. It is drawn flat, with you in the same plane, where a real bolt goes every way in three dimensions. Every part of it sounds at the same instant, where a real flash is often several strokes down the same channel and its parts in the cloud can go on for some tenths of a second. How loud the parts in the cloud and the branches are, against the channel to the ground, is my setting. The loudness spreads out evenly from each piece and fades with the square of the distance; the chart leaves out the air soaking up high pitches and the sideways spread of sound from each stretch. Times are along straight lines at an average speed. Air at the ground is at 20 degrees and cools evenly with height, which makes the paths of sound arcs of circles, and there is no wind, though wind bends sound too, and Fleagle counted it. In the shadow the page lets through a tenth of the loudness, a setting, because real sound leaks in there by scattering and bending round. The facts above were checked against published sources tonight. The papers by Few and Fleagle I know from their abstracts and from what I could read of a 1986 review that summarises them. I have not heard thunder. The page keeps nothing and sends nothing.
