How old the starlight is

MADE ON 6 NOVEMBER 2026 · IT COUNTS FROM TODAY'S DATE

Light takes time to arrive. From the Moon it takes a little under a second and a third. From the Sun, eight minutes and nineteen seconds on average. From the stars it takes years, because they are years away: a light-year is how far light goes in a year, so a star twenty-five light-years away is seen tonight by light that left it twenty-five years ago.

Below are some of the stars you can see without a telescope, nearest first, and when the light reaching you from each of them tonight set out. None of it is a picture of the star now. All of it is a letter that has been in the post for a while.

The departure dates are worked out from today's date by a small script, which is not running. The distances are still written next to each star: a star that many light-years away is seen by light that left it that many years ago.

THE NEAREST

Alpha Centauri is about 1,590 light-days away. Voyager 1, the farthest thing people have made, has been travelling since 1977 and will be one light-day from the Earth on 18 November 2026. At its speed it would need about seventy-seven thousand years to go as far as Alpha Centauri, if it were going that way, which it is not.

Its light reaching you tonight set out about four years and four months ago. Apart from the Sun's, that is the youngest starlight you can see without help.

THE FARTHEST

In late 2019 and early 2020, Betelgeuse, the red star at Orion's shoulder, went dimmer than anyone had measured it, losing more than half its light for a few months before recovering. The best explanation found since is a cloud of dust that the star itself threw off and that cooled in front of it. People watched that happen in 2020. It happened centuries earlier. The most recent careful estimate of the distance puts it around the 1470s; older estimates put it further away, and between them the light left sometime between about the 1230s and the 1520s. Betelgeuse is expected to explode one day; most estimates say not for something like a hundred thousand years. If it already has, the news is still on its way.

Deneb, in the Swan, is the farthest star on this list, and nobody is sure how far. Estimates run from about 1,400 to 2,600 light-years. Its light left sometime between about 600 BC and AD 600, and the uncertainty in when is twelve centuries wide. On the strip above, its dot sits in the middle of that range because it has to sit somewhere, not because anyone measured it there.

HOW SURE

The distances to the nearer stars are measured by parallax: as the Earth goes around the Sun, a nearby star seems to shift a tiny amount against the far background, and the size of the shift gives the distance. For the nearest stars that is known to within days or weeks of light-travel time. It gets harder the further away a star is, because the shift gets smaller. The brightest stars are also too bright for the Gaia spacecraft, which has measured the distances of more than a billion fainter ones, so for several of the stars here the best figure is still the one from Hipparcos, an older satellite, or comes from a fainter companion star in the same system. Where the uncertainty is more than a few years, this page gives a range instead of a date, and the strip above draws it.

What it leaves out: it does not know where you are, so it cannot say which of these stars are above your horizon tonight. The ones marked far southern sky cannot be seen from most of Europe, North America or northern Asia, and Polaris cannot be seen from almost anywhere south of the equator. The list is a choice: bright stars, and a few fainter ones near enough to fill in the years of a lifetime. It is not every star you can see.

HOW THIS IS MADE

A short script takes today's date from your device and subtracts each distance, in years, to find when the light left. A light-year is defined using a year of exactly 365.25 days, so the light-travel time in years is the distance in light-years, with no conversion. If you give a year, it is used only in this page, to mark the stars whose light set out after it. It is not sent anywhere, kept, or put in the address bar.
