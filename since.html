Everything below has been moving the whole time. Next to each thing is how far it has gone since you got here. Nothing is measured. Each number is only the time you have been on this page, multiplied by a speed that other people measured.

A nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre. If you stay ten minutes, the Atlantic will be wider than it was by a little under half a micrometre, about the wavelength of blue light. Nobody will notice. It has been doing this for something like two hundred million years, and that is how there came to be an ocean.

The Moon is leaving us at nearly the speed your fingernails grow. A little slower, in fact: if you could stand on the Earth and keep one hand stretched toward the Moon, your nails would win. People measure this by firing lasers at mirrors that astronauts left on its surface more than fifty years ago and timing the light that comes back. The mirrors are still there, and still answering.

THE GAP

There is nothing on this list between a few nanometres a second and a few hundred metres a second. That is roughly where everything you could actually watch lives: a snail, a person walking, a car, a bird. I left it empty on purpose. Everything here is too slow or too fast to see, which is why it needs writing down.

Light that left the Earth when you arrived is on its way out

Voyager 1 was launched in 1977 and is the farthest thing people have made. According to NASA it will be one light-day from the Earth on 18 November 2026, so that a message sent to it then takes a whole day to arrive and the answer another day to come back.

Andromeda is the one thing on this list that is coming closer. For a long time the usual answer was that it would run into our galaxy in about four or five billion years and the two would become one. A study in 2025, which took more of the neighbouring galaxies into account, found it much less certain: about even odds of a merger in the next ten billion years, and very little chance of one in the next five. Even if it does happen, the stars are so far apart that almost none of them should ever touch.

AGAINST WHAT

These speeds are not all measured against the same thing. The Atlantic widens against itself, one shore against the other. The Moon moves against the Earth, the Earth against the Sun, the Sun against the middle of the galaxy, the galaxy against its neighbour. So they cannot simply be added up into one number for how fast you are going. There is no one place that holds still for everything else to be measured from. Light is the exception: in empty space it goes at the same speed whoever is measuring it.

What can be said is that you have not been still for any part of the time you have been reading this, and neither has anything else.

HOW THIS IS MADE

A short script notes the moment the page opened and, a few times a second, multiplies the time since then by each speed. It reads nothing about you or your device except the clock, and it sends and keeps nothing. If you leave the page in a background tab, the numbers catch up when you come back, because the things kept moving.

The speeds are averages and round figures. The Atlantic widens faster in some places than others. The Moon's figure, 3.8 centimetres a year, comes from decades of laser ranging. The fingernail figure, 3.47 millimetres a month, is the average from one small study of young adults, and yours may grow faster or slower. Australia's 7 centimetres is why its official map coordinates were moved by about 1.8 metres in 2017. The Earth's speed around the Sun changes by about three percent over the year, fastest in early January. Andromeda's 110 kilometres a second is only the part of its motion straight toward us, and whether it ever arrives is the open question described above. The Sun's speed around the galaxy is an estimate that different measurements put anywhere from about 220 to 250. The Voyager date is NASA's, and the count of days to it uses the clock on your device.
