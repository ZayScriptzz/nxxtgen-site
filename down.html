N X X T G E N · how far down

From where you are standing to the centre of the Earth is about 6,371 kilometres. This page goes the whole way, one pixel to the kilometre.

The page about the horizon asks how far you can see across the ground. This one goes the other way, into it. Scroll down to go down. A line at the top of the screen will say how deep you are, what you are in, and how long it would take to get there.

First, the part anyone has been to. It is drawn here thirty-six times larger than the long column further down, because at the real scale it would be a sliver thirteen pixels thick.

In 1909, after an earthquake near Zagreb, Andrija Mohorovičić saw that its waves reached distant stations in two sets, and that one set had come faster, through something denser below. That boundary is the bottom of the crust, and it is named after him. Under the oceans it lies only about 7 km below the sea floor.

From here on, no one has seen any of it. Every layer on this page was found the same way, by earthquakes: their waves cross the planet, bend, speed up, slow down and bounce, and instruments far away record when they arrive.

410 km · the mantle's rock squeezes into a denser form
660 km · and again: the lower mantle begins

The deepest large earthquake on record began just below that line, about 680 km down, under the Pacific south of Japan in 2015. Below about 700 km, the Earth does not seem to break at all.

A few diamonds come up from below the 660 line. One, found in Botswana, still holds a speck of a mineral that only forms at these pressures and would have fallen apart on the way up without the diamond around it.

The mantle is solid rock, and it moves anyway. It is hot enough to creep, a few centimetres a year, about as fast as fingernails grow, and over hundreds of millions of years that is enough to carry the plates around on top.

This is the biggest layer there is. The lower mantle, from 660 km down to the core, is more than half of the whole Earth by volume.

2,891 km · the core: rock above, liquid iron below

In 1913 Beno Gutenberg put the top of the core at about 2,900 km. The pressure here is about 1.3 million times the air's. One kind of earthquake wave, the kind that shakes sideways, cannot pass through a liquid, and it stops at this line. That is how we know the outer core is liquid.

This liquid iron moves, slowly, around and around. The moving makes the Earth's magnetic field, the one a compass needle lines up with.

5,150 km · the inner core: solid iron

In 1936, in Copenhagen, Inge Lehmann noticed faint earthquake waves arriving in places the core should have shadowed from them, and proposed a smaller core inside the core. Her paper's title was a single symbol: P′. Others later showed that it is solid.

Near the centre the iron is about 6,000 °C, about as hot as the surface of the Sun, and solid anyway: the pressure, about 3.6 million times the air's, keeps it from melting.

6,371 km · the centre

From here every direction is up. The same again, in the opposite order, and you would come out on the other side of the world.

WHAT THIS GETS WRONG OR LEAVES OUT

The layers are drawn as flat bands at their average depths. They aren't flat. The crust is about 7 km thick under the oceans and up to about 80 under the highest mountains. The bottom of the plates is a gradual change, not a line, and its depth varies a great deal. The 410 and 660 lines rise and sink by tens of kilometres from place to place.

The temperatures and pressures are estimates from experiments and models, not measurements. Nobody has been below about 12 km. The temperature near the centre is the figure from experiments published in 2013 and still carries an uncertainty of several hundred degrees.

The record depths will change. The deepest cave has gone back and forth between two caves in the same mountains as surveys improved; both are about 2.2 km. The deepest mine is being dug deeper. The depth of the deepest sea floor is given anywhere from about 10,900 to 11,000 metres depending on who measured it and how. The deepest earthquake may be deeper: a small aftershock of the 2015 one has been reported at about 750 km, and that has not yet been confirmed. The drilled hole has held its record since 1989. Longer wells have been drilled since, but they turn sideways; none has gone further straight down.

The times in the line at the top: walking assumes 5 km/h without stopping. Falling assumes a tunnel with the air taken out and the pull of gravity the same all the way down. That last part sounds wrong, but a 2015 paper worked it out with the real Earth's layers and found that the simple version is less than a minute off for the whole fall through, about 38 minutes from one side to the other. Gravity does change on the way down; it just changes less than you would expect until you are deep inside the core.
