In the north, the earliest sunset of the year comes before the shortest day, and the latest sunrise comes after it.

At 45° north in the winter of 2026 into 2027, the earliest sunset is on 10 December, the shortest day is 21 December, and the latest sunrise is on 2 January.

At 45° north the December solstice falls on 21 December, and these times are by the clock. The earliest sunset is on 10 December, 11 days before it, at 16:18; on the solstice the sun sets 2.8 minutes later than that. The latest sunrise is on 2 January, 12 days after it, at 07:38; on the solstice the sun rises 3.2 minutes earlier than that. At the solstice noon by the sun comes about 30 seconds later on the clock each day, wherever you are.

WHY

A clock gives every day exactly twenty-four hours. A sundial counts a day from one noon to the next, noon being the moment the sun stands due south, or due north south of the equator. The two kinds of day are not the same length. In late December a day from noon to noon by the sun is about twenty-four hours and thirty seconds, so each day noon by the sun comes about half a minute later on the clock. The dashed line in the drawing is that drift.

Sunset is noon by the sun plus half the daylight. Through the first three weeks of December the afternoons are still getting shorter, but by less each day as the solstice nears, and soon they are shrinking by less than the half minute that noon slides later. From then on each sunset is a little later than the one before, though the days are still getting shorter. Sunrise is noon minus half the daylight, and it goes the other way: after the solstice the mornings start to lengthen, slowly at first, more slowly than noon is sliding later, so sunrise keeps getting later into January.

By a sundial none of this happens. Press the sundial button and the dashed line goes flat, and the earliest sunset and the latest sunrise both move to the shortest day. The whole effect belongs to the clock, which keeps an average sun instead of the real one.

It is small. At 45° north the sun sets on the shortest day less than three minutes later than on the earliest evening, and for about two weeks in the first half of December sunset stays within a minute of its earliest, which is why a table of sunsets to the minute shows the same time day after day. Move the latitude and it changes. Far north the days change length quickly, the drift of noon matters less, and the earliest sunset comes within a few days of the solstice. Near the equator the length of the day hardly changes at all, so sunset follows noon by the sun almost exactly, and the earliest sunset of the year is in early November, when the sundial is furthest ahead of the clock.

South of the equator December is midsummer, and the same arithmetic gives the earliest sunrise before the solstice and the latest sunset after it. Around the June solstice it all happens again, the other way round in each hemisphere, but more weakly: in June noon by the sun drifts only about thirteen seconds a day, not thirty, for a reason the next part explains.

A YEAR OF NOONS

If you stood in the same place and marked where the sun is at noon by the clock on every day of a year, the marks would make this figure. It runs up and down with the seasons, 47 degrees from the June solstice at the top to the December one at the bottom. It runs left and right because the sundial is sometimes ahead of the clock and sometimes behind. Up to about sixteen and a half minutes ahead in early November, when the sun has already passed south at clock noon and stands to the west, and about fourteen minutes behind in mid February, when it has not reached south yet.

A minute of time is a quarter of a degree of the sky, so the figure is about seven and a half degrees wide, fourteen of the sun's own widths. It is drawn here at its true shape, with the sun's disc to scale beside it. The two loops are not the same size, and that is the clue to there being two reasons, not one. Seen from the south of the equator, facing north, it is turned over.

TWO REASONS

  • sundial ahead of the clock
  • from the tilt
  • from the orbit
  • in minutes

The orbit. The Earth's path round the Sun is not quite a circle, and it moves fastest when it is closest, in the first days of January, on the third in 2027. Each day it moves the Sun a little further east against the stars, and the Earth has to turn a little further than one full turn to bring the Sun back to the south. When the Earth is moving fastest that extra turn is largest, and a day from noon to noon is about eight seconds longer than average. In early July, at the far end of the orbit, it is about eight seconds shorter. On its own this would put the sundial up to about seven and a half minutes either side of the clock, once a year.

The tilt. The Sun's yearly path across the sky is tilted about 23.4 degrees to the equator, and a clock counts only how far east it goes, measured along the equator. At the equinoxes the path crosses the equator at a slant, so part of each day's movement is north or south and doesn't count, and the day from noon to noon is about twenty seconds shorter than average. At the solstices the path runs level, the Sun is far from the equator where the hours of the sky are closer together, and the same day's movement counts for more, about twenty seconds more. On its own this would put the sundial up to about ten minutes either side of the clock, twice a year, which is why the figure has two loops.

In December both make the day longer: about twenty-two seconds from the tilt and eight from the orbit, together the half minute a day that moves the earliest sunset off the shortest day. In June the tilt still makes the day longer, but the orbit, near its slowest, makes it shorter, and the two together give only about thirteen seconds. That is why the earliest sunset sits further from the solstice in a northern winter than in a southern one. It is also why the bottom loop of the figure, the northern winter, is the wide one.

WHO NOTICED

Ptolemy knew both reasons. In the third book of the Almagest, in the second century, he explains that days from noon to noon are unequal because the sun moves unevenly along its path, and because equal stretches of that path do not cross the meridian in equal times, and he allows for both.

For most of the time since, it mattered to astronomers more than to anyone else, until clocks were steady enough to keep the average sun. Huygens, who built the first pendulum clock, published a table in 1665 for setting a clock by the sun, in a booklet on using clocks to find longitude at sea; it is counted the first essentially correct one. Flamsteed published his in 1673, worked from both reasons together.

Towns went on keeping the sun's time long after that, and moved their public clocks to the average one at different dates. Berlin did it in 1810 and Paris in 1826. Geneva's clockmakers worked to the average sun from 1778, and its public clocks changed in 1821. England was probably first, but the most recent history I found could not pin down when. The figure of eight is credited to Grandjean de Fouchy, who drew it on the noon line of a sundial around 1730, so that the shadow could be read as clock time. The first photograph of it was made by Dennis di Cicco in Watertown, Massachusetts, between February 1978 and February 1979: the sun, about forty-four times over the year, on a single frame of film.

WHAT IT LEAVES OUT

The times are clock times on the middle line of a time zone, without summer time. Your own clock is four minutes off that for every degree of longitude you are from the middle of your zone, and an hour off in summer time. Both shift every sunrise and sunset by the same amount, so they change the times on this page but not the dates. The sun's position comes from a short formula good to about a minute of arc, which is a few seconds of time. The horizon is flat and at sea level, and the air bends the light by the same usual amount every evening; real sunsets vary by a minute or more with the weather alone. The dates of the earliest and latest are the single days the formula picks, and because the curves are so flat at the bottom, a day either way is within the accuracy of all of it. The page does not ask where you are, and nothing is sent or kept. The latitude you choose goes into the address bar, so a link carries it, and nowhere else.
